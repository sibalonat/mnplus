# Who Do You Call About the Machine

I think we heard about last week news, when two companies that are trying to file for an IPO, both of them, and as if it were coordinated, came out to say they want to slow AI development down. It was not clear the roadmap of this slowdown nor what it would entail, I mean, aside from the marketing standpoint. But there are many people who take these things very seriously, as it is the source of their content. I could not avoid starting this article with the news either, so here it is. I am not writing about it because I want to talk about it. I already avoid engaging too much with news that exists to create fear. The discussion around this fear is very strongly voiced, it is almost two weeks now, and the blog posts and interviews of these tech leaders will most likely keep echoing for a while.

So I was thinking about it — AGI, the end of the human race, all of it — one day around lunchtime, while I was getting ready to pick up my daughter from the pre-kindergarten where she now stays until lunch.

## A Phone With Labels On It

I was in one of my rooms at home, the one i have for an office. On a shelf there is a very old phone with a dial. Pale yellow. I have had it for years and I do not think I had ever really looked at it. That day, while I was getting my keys, I took a long look.

The dial is the kind you have to rotate almost a full circle to pick each number. I was used to thinking of these phones in one way — a dial, ten holes, that is it. But this one has labels. In French, I think. Around the dial, more or less making a full circle, there are the services you could call, written next to the numbers: _renseignements_ (information), _réclamations_ (complaints), _télégraphe_, _police-secours_ (emergency police), _pompiers_ (the fire brigade), and _intérêt régional (voir annuaire)_ — regional services, see the directory.

I do not know how, maybe because I had not remembered that these labels were there at all, but the thought that came was: look, these are the most important services a person could need to call in an emergency. Somebody sat down and decided which six things deserve to be written on the phone itself. Not the number of your friend. You cannot write your friend's name there. These are numbers that are not as present in your life as a friend is, but they are there in case you need them. A lesson learned from history — from enough kitchens on fire and enough strangers at the door — turned into a label that is important exactly when you require it, and invisible the rest of the time.

## Good or Bad, Slow or Fast

I read a lot of Noam Chomsky when I was young. There is something he describes, a theory whose name I do not remember, and it goes more or less like this: television, media in general, is a means that serves to make separation, and it does it through dichotomy labels — good or bad, us or them — and that is the thing it does best.

This war now, between slowing down and speeding up, is one of those labels. It keeps the conversation about AI going. And the conversation has to keep going, because we need to spend. I personally spend, on the median, somewhere between a hundred and two hundred dollars a month on AI usage. A while ago I wanted to understand whether I could learn anything from that usage — the train of thought it had followed to arrive at a specific conclusion, the why behind the answer. There is nothing there. Some strings of session ids, something about how this conversation is interpreted by the platform that created the session, probably through a key id. That is the whole receipt.

## Labelled As Made By A Machine

How I learned about the key id is funny. In an earlier article I wrote that the EU will start enforcing that AI-made content is labelled on the European market. I was reading about this, and I learned that there are algorithms you can run to analyse whether a piece of content was made by AI. They only interpret specific patterns, and cross-reference the pattern with word usage and sentence structure, and from that they decide whether this was created by a machine.

At a certain point I thought I could build a tool to help people with this, after reading that an English politician and a Swedish one were using AI to write their speeches. And who is not. It ended up that you needed the whole story of how a person writes or talks to actually do that. Which in it's self made the idea of the tooling, not useful - and Claude suggested it was dangerous(legally). But we are using these tools and I trusted Claude on this assesment. It is fair. Everybody who's been using these tools, trusts them. For example, my Italian proficiency is averagely good on speaking basis, but I struggle on written basis. And while sometimes aim to write to my friends for stuff, I struggle therefore its easier to use Claude or other tools. Sometimes I think Claude is good(at all context of usage), and then I learn that to make the text look more human-written you can pass it through something else, Gemini for example. But I am a person whose impression phase passes really fast. I am impressed in one moment, and when I read it again the next day I think it is more useless than I expected.

So there is a label for content: made by a machine, or not. And there is a label for the companies: slowing down, or speeding up. What I could not find, anywhere in my own usage, was a label for the thing I actually wanted to know — what did it think, why it was sure, on the connection it made about the information it gave me.

## A Dragon On The Left

Sometimes, after work, I take a long walk to a place near home. I call it a park, but it is really the start of a hiking trail. It is September now, so obviously, having finished work at six, it gets relatively dark soon. Walking is the best time I have for thinking about things, about the future and the rest.

This time was no different, but sure enough, while I was walking, it got darker and darker, and it became difficult to spot things clearly. The leaves around the trail were almost black. The ground, if it did not have that stony colour with some flavour of sand in it, would have been black as well. And while I was walking I thought: what if now, for example, a dragon came out. From the left. And I had to face it.

While I was thinking about this I got stuck on a specific thing. Not only what the dragon would do to me, but how I would think of myself in relation to this creature — as its tamer, or as its prey. The question came naturally. Because in the interpretation we have of the current situation, we think of ourselves as tamers, and I assume we like to feel that fortune has bigger plans for us. But if I were in front of the dragon, now, on this trail, how much of this conscious self-determination would matter for my survival.

That is the dichotomy label again, only this time the media did not hand it to me. I did that myself, in the dark, in about four seconds. Tamer or prey. And the honest answer was that neither word was going to help me, and that the only useful thought I had was: which way is the road, and who do I call.

## Human Oversight, Written On The Dial

And then the question I have been circling for a few posts now — where do you place the decision: in the machine's hand, under human oversight, or with a human in the loop.

The phone is the honest version of the answer. The six labels on it were not written by whoever was selling phones that year. They were written after the fact, by people who had been there — after the fire, after the theft, after the telegram that did not arrive. The label is the lesson, and the lesson costs something before it becomes a label. The slow-or-fast label is the opposite kind. It was written before anything happened, to keep us talking and spending, and it tells you nothing about what to do at two in the morning when something comes out from the left.

Human oversight, I think, is the part in between. It is the person who stands there before the label exists, when the phone company has not decided yet which number you dial for this, and who has to go and look anyway. It is not a heroic role. It is mostly walking back down a dark trail with a stony ground, checking that the thing which came back is the thing you asked for, and writing down what happened so that someone else gets a label out of it.

When I was thinking about the end of this article I had different ideas of how to end it. But I looked again at the pale yellow phone and its labels, and I imagined that there would be a seventh number, one for the machine. What would it be called, and based on which lessons. What did we learn, and did we learn it before or after the fire.

I did not have the answer. I had to go and pick up my daughter.

### Try It: The Number Nobody Wrote Down

Here is the phone, more or less. Around the dial there are five labels — **RENSEIGNEMENTS**, **RÉCLAMATIONS**, **TÉLÉGRAPHE**, **POLICE-SECOURS**, **POMPIERS** — and a sixth one that is blank. Things happen through the evening, and each one arrives as a card at the top: smoke from the kitchen, a stranger at the door, a bill that is twice what it should be. Each card has its own patience, and it runs out whether you are on the phone or not. **Dial the right label** and it is handled. Dial the wrong one and a polite voice says _not our number_, and you have lost the time it took the dial to come back around. Then the other kind of card starts arriving: _the machine wrote the letter and signed your name_, _the machine says the road is clear, it is not_. There is no label for these. Every service you dial will tell you so. The only thing that works is the blank label — dial it and you put the phone down and go and look yourself, which takes long, and while you are out in the dark every other card keeps losing patience. Three things going bad ends the evening. When it is over, look at what the game wrote on the sixth label, because it writes it from what you did.

**The point?** The first evening goes the way you would expect. You learn the five labels fast, because they were made to be learned: smoke is the fire brigade, a stranger is the police, a wrong bill is complaints, and the dial comes back around before anything gets too bad. What you do not learn fast is the sixth one. The first time a card about the machine arrives you dial the police, because it feels like a danger, and the police say it is not their number. Then the fire brigade, because you smell something. Then information, because at least somebody might know what it is. Each time the dial has to come all the way back around, and the kitchen card behind it is quietly running out of patience. The only thing that ever works is the blank label — putting the phone down and going to look yourself — and it costs more than any call, because while you are out there nobody is answering anything. That is what the pale yellow phone kept saying to me while I stood in my room with the keys in my hand: every label on it was paid for by someone who had to go and look before the number existed. The machine will not get its label from a press release about slowing down. It will get it from the people who are still willing to walk out into the dark, check what came back, and write down what they found. Be one of them, and notice what the game wrote on your sixth label — it is the lesson you were actually teaching it.
