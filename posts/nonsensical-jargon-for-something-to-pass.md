# Nonsensical Jargon for Something to Pass

While I enjoy reading, I have been experimenting with audiobooks. For some types of books, it's easier to just consume them via headsets. I was recently listening to *"Fashionable Nonsense"* written by Alan Sokal and Jean Bricmont. In this book, both authors shed light on the lasting effects of postmodernism and how experimentation with the structure of sentences and usage of words without proper context has created some of the most fantastical and most absurd statements philosophers have made since postmodernism became a major philosophical approach - and probably still is.

The context Sokal brings to the story is the "hoax" article published in *Social Text*, where he submitted a nonsensical paper filled with scientific jargon to test whether it would be accepted - and in fact, the paper was accepted. The reason I bring postmodernism into this article is not that I know a lot about it. In fact, postmodernist writers are the ones I have read the least.

## A Personal Confession

But that doesn't mean I'm a person who respects much of grammatical jargon in my native language, Albanian. That is because I have not been a good student when I was a kid, and until I was studying in university, I hadn't liked grammar very much. Things changed later, when I learned to love writing - there was a period when I was constantly writing.

The reason I mention this is that while I was listening (almost wrote "reading") to this audiobook, I was thinking of all the times I'm home with my wife and daughter, and the jokes I make. In most cases, these jokes are about my usage of words from other areas of the country or different dialects. Although my wife cracks up laughing every time, she corrects me every time about the correct usage of words.

> **Helper Note**: The "Sokal Affair" occurred in 1996 when physicist Alan Sokal submitted a deliberately nonsensical paper titled "Transgressing the Boundaries: Towards a Transformative Hermeneutics of Quantum Gravity" to the cultural studies journal *Social Text*. The paper was written as a parody, using fashionable postmodern jargon without any real meaning, yet it was accepted and published. This became a famous critique of postmodernism's alleged lack of intellectual rigor.

## The Shifting Landscape

While I am still thinking about the entire process of shifting from traditional software development careers to new ones, where agility and adaptation to the situation is crucial, I can't stop connecting these thoughts.

When I would hear people mention postmodernism so passionately, I would always recall that maybe our way of thinking changes with the passing of time. With that, words, terms, and structures tend to change or are interpreted differently whenever an authority either decides to change the interpretation, or a domain changes priorities. Therefore, the interpretation of certain things - like what is a software developer and what is AI - changes.

I remember one day, an AI engineer who works with me said to me while I was joking about the happiness of using AI:

"It's not AI, it's Machine Learning."

I can't say I'm confused by the difference in terms. I'm old enough to remember the literature-like aspects of AI and the Turing test, and the contextual work when ML was introduced - I believe as working prototypes around 2014 by Google with images.

> **Helper Note**: The distinction between "AI" and "Machine Learning" is important: AI is the broader concept of machines performing tasks that would require human intelligence, while ML is a specific subset focusing on systems that learn from data. Google's major ML breakthrough with images refers to the 2012 AlexNet deep learning model that won the ImageNet competition, followed by Google's 2014-2015 advancements in image recognition using deep neural networks.

## The Ease of Creation

It's baffling that we are able to create so much work so easily. For developers, it has opened our minds to what is possible and the usage - me working with instruction files, managing tokens, prompting correctly, and making test-ready applications.

We have shifted our work to make it easier for years - through frameworks, documentation, tools, testing, and now AI. We can't act surprised by how this has happened.

I remember one episode during the time of COVID: the father of the product owner of a project was hearing about one of the requirements for a new dev we needed, and he jumped in to say that he "could do it" because he was reading some books about it. Probably not the primary reason he couldn't join the team, but I think what his son was hearing was that if you have the wrong means to learn, then you can't possibly be that agile to adapt. And the time now requires adaptability more than anything.

## Two Projects, Two Approaches

Currently, I'm involved in two projects: one is work, and the other is personal. The one from work has very specific requirements - at least considering it's an MVP. While I have always detested agentic code (or "vibe coding") and used simple chat, I sometimes found it difficult to work with. I eventually pulled the trigger and threw my agentic coding assistant into action.

I sometimes think that software development requirements are crucial, mainly for two reasons:

1. **Context limitations**: The AI doesn't have all the necessary information about the process during code creation. Even if it has some context, it sometimes skips or fails to understand the entire picture. Imagine something running with 300 errors - it always requires help to enforce and make sure that everything is on the rails.

2. **Human oversight**: We need to be the ones who understand the business logic, the edge cases, the user experience. AI can generate code, but it's our responsibility to ensure it makes sense in the larger context.

> **Helper Note**: "Agentic code" or "agentic AI" refers to AI systems that can autonomously plan and execute multi-step tasks with minimal human intervention, like GitHub Copilot's workspace feature or Cursor's Composer. "Vibe coding" is a somewhat tongue-in-cheek term for letting AI generate large amounts of code based on high-level descriptions without detailed specifications.

## The Postmodern Parallel

This brings me back to the Sokal affair. Just as postmodernist texts could pass peer review despite being nonsensical because they used the right jargon, AI-generated code can sometimes pass initial review because it looks right - it has the proper syntax, uses the right libraries, follows certain patterns. But without human oversight, without someone who understands the deeper context, that code might be as meaningless as Sokal's fake paper.

The difference is: we can't afford to accept nonsensical jargon in our codebase just because it sounds sophisticated or because an AI wrote it with confidence. We need to be the gatekeepers, the ones who ensure that what passes through actually makes sense.

Just like my wife corrects my Albanian dialect jokes, we developers need to correct the AI when it generates code that might compile and run, but doesn't actually solve the problem correctly or elegantly.

## Adaptability as the New Literacy

The ability to morph, to adapt, to learn new ways of working - this is the polymorphism we all need now. Not just in code, but in how we approach our careers, our tools, and our relationship with AI.

The grammatical rules I once ignored in school? They matter when you're trying to communicate clearly. The distinctions between AI and ML that seemed pedantic? They matter when you're architecting systems. The requirements that seemed tedious? They matter when you're ensuring the AI doesn't generate elegant nonsense.

We're in a period where adaptation isn't just valuable - it's essential. And part of that adaptation is learning when to trust the new tools and when to question them, when to accept the jargon and when to demand clarity.

> **Final Helper Note**: This article connects several threads: the postmodern tendency to use complex language without clear meaning, the author's personal experience with language and grammar, and the current state of AI-assisted development. The central metaphor is that just as Sokal's hoax exposed how academic jargon can mask meaninglessness, AI-generated code can look correct while lacking true understanding - making human oversight crucial.

## What This Means for Us

We can't go back to the time before AI tools. We can't pretend that the old ways were perfect or that the new ways are inherently problematic. What we can do is maintain our role as the interpreters, the translators between what the tools generate and what actually needs to exist.

Sometimes the AI will be brilliant. Sometimes it will generate something that passes all the tests but makes no sense architecturally. Our job is to know the difference - and to keep learning so we can continue to know the difference as the tools evolve.

The jargon will keep changing. The tools will keep evolving. But our responsibility to ensure that what passes through actually makes sense? That remains constant.
