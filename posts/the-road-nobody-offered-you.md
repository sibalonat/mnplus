# The Road Nobody Offered You

We were away with the family from the second week of August until the middle of the third. The plan had two halves. First the north — a place called Fierzë, where we had found a very nice family guesthouse, the kind that stays popular all year round and not only in August, and which sits close to Shala, one of those places you are told at least once a year that you have to see. Then we would pivot back down to Vlora.

There was another reason I wanted it in that order. I have never really done the long stretch drives, the ones that could plausibly cover the entire country in one go. So this looked like a good occasion to cross places we had never crossed, and maybe to read something out of the crossing itself — the realities that a road between two points tends to tell you about, whether or not you asked it to.

## Four Hours on Paper

I had put together, more or less, an itinerary of a four-hour drive from the capital to the guesthouse. What I had not done was open Google Maps and look at the route it would actually choose. And so I ended up assuming — without ever checking — that it was normal for the thing to take around six.

We left early, mostly to stay ahead of the traffic that builds at that time of year. And I decided that this drive would be different from our weekend drives, where we put music on. This time we would listen to a book: *Guardrails — Guiding Human Decisions in the Age of AI*, by Urs Gasser and Viktor Mayer-Schönberger.

## The Book Opens With a Crash

It opens dramatically, which I did not expect. A plane, a landing that has to be pivoted in a very unconventional way, and two authorities sitting in the same loop: the control tower on one side, the automated system on the other. Each one overwrote the other's decision. What came out of that was a crash.

I was listening to that part in the middle of traffic. And traffic in Tirana is quite bothersome, because it asks for a lot of attention. There are drivers who think it is a fine idea to overtake, or to pass in very close proximity, and for people not living in Albania this is a first shock — then they get used to it surprisingly fast, because as they say, it happens without shouting, without drama, and without (often) accidents.

While I was steering, I saw a car in front of me driving almost into a section of the street where it was not allowed to be, and it had crossed the line to move into a new lane, even though nothing about that was permitted. At the same time I was watching the screen near the steering wheel, checking whether I was still going in the right direction. So there it was: a book about two systems overwriting each other, a car ahead of me overwriting the line on the road, and my own eyes going back and forth to a small screen that was telling me where to be.

## The Car in Front of You

That is when I started thinking about the drive that a condition presents.

When I was learning to drive, one of the first things that stuck with me — and it has stuck since — was to check the proximity of the car in front of me. I do that always. But as it happens, checking for proximity is not the only thing going on. I notice sometimes that there is also a drive to *follow* the car in front of you. It is not that it happens often. But I cannot hide that, more likely than not, it is something that happens.

And there I was thinking that following Google Maps, or following cars, is something that triggers an expected behaviour. An oriented one. Something that is supposed to happen. An expectation of reality.

## An Expectation That Did Not Hold

Except that is not what happened to the plane in the book. Two authorities, each one correct inside its own frame, each one expecting the other to behave the way it was oriented to behave — and the expectation did not hold. It is not what happened with self-driving cars either. And it is not what happens with the AI building platforms that arrive with the promise of being better than the ones that have not been updated yet.

I can say that things are now changing much faster. We are updating stacks, technologies and opportunities at a pace that would have looked unserious a few years ago. Maybe this is also a grace period, because most of us as developers have had about one year to get used to this reality, and one year is not nothing. You can build a rhythm in a year. You can build an expectation in a year.

But there is also the other case, and it is the one I kept turning over between Tirana and the north: that the choices we are presented with are not real.

## The Options Are Not Real

The AI that overwrote the command to land the plane presented a choice. The UI that an AI proposed for the platform I am building with my brother presented a choice — a clean set of options, laid out as if those were the ones that existed. The self-driving car that concluded a specific course of action would be accident-free presented a choice. In each case, we are only assuming some predefined conditions under which the machine is error-free, and then we treat the menu it hands us as the shape of the world.

It is the same instinct as following the car in front. The car in front is not a route. It is a car. It happens to be going somewhere, and its being there does not make its line the line. But proximity does something to you, and after a while the thing you are checking becomes the thing you are following.

That, for me, is where the risk sits now, and it is also the challenge: not to restrict our options to the ones the AI assumes are the only ones. Because the moment we accept the menu as the territory, we are not just narrowing our own work. We may be actively empowering the people who never accepted the menu in the first place.

## Who Is Left Off the Menu

Bad actors do not rely much on predefined or preselected options. That is more or less the definition of the job. If everyone in a system converges on the routes that were suggested to them — the same stack, the same defaults, the same generated shape of a screen, the same predicted safe course — then the person who deliberately takes the road nobody offered is operating in a space that has quietly been emptied of everyone else.

I keep coming back to the same conclusion in these posts, and I did not go looking for it this time either; it was waiting for me on the road north, with a car in front of me. Human oversight is not a fence you put around the machine. It is the standing refusal to let the presented options become the only options. Every action should be intentional, and an intentional action includes the one that was never lit up for you on the screen.

The drive, by the way, took a good deal more than the four hours I had written down. Which was fine. We crossed places we had never crossed, which was the entire point of going that way, and none of them were on the itinerary I had planned.

### Try It: The Lane Nobody Lit

Here is the drive turned into something you can hold in your hands. Three lanes. At every junction the navigation lights two of them **SUGGESTED** in gold, and leaves one dark, marked with nothing but a question mark. The lit lanes move you one junction closer to the guesthouse. The dark lane gives you no progress at all — it is the long way round — but it drops your **Convergence** and gives you back **Margin**. It is also never the lane you are already sitting in, so taking it always costs you a deliberate move. There is also a lead car ahead of you, drifting between lanes; sit in its lane and your Convergence climbs faster, because that is what tailing is. And the higher Convergence goes, the more often a lit lane turns out to be **CLOSED** — the route rewritten under you, and revealed so late that you have about a second to move. Past seventy percent, both lit lanes can close at once, and the only lane left standing is the one nobody lit. You have twenty junctions to cover and a daylight bar that does not wait for you. So you cannot take the dark lane every time either. That is the whole game: it is not free to keep an option nobody offered you, and it is not survivable to stop keeping one.

**The point?** The first runs go the way my drive went. The gold lanes are right there, they are clearly the fast way, and taking them feels like nothing more than reading the screen correctly — which it is, right up until the screen changes its mind at the last moment and there is nowhere left to put the car. The dark lane never looks like the answer, because it is marked with a question mark and it costs you the one thing you are being scored on. But it is the only lane that is never rewritten under you, and the only reason you still have somewhere to go when both of the offered ones close. That is what I could not stop thinking about with a car in front of me and a small screen to the right of the wheel: the suggestion is not the road, and the car ahead is not the route. We are the ones who decide that following is the same as arriving. Keep one option nobody offered you — it will cost you time, it will cost you progress, and one day it will be the only lane still open.
