I planned a four-hour drive north with my family this August. It took six. I had never opened the map to check the route it would pick — I just assumed six was normal.

We spent the drive listening to Guardrails, by Urs Gasser and Viktor Mayer-Schönberger. It opens with a crash: a landing that had to be pivoted an unconventional way, a control tower on one side, an automated system on the other, each one overwriting the other's decision.

I was hearing that in traffic when the car in front of me crossed a line it was not allowed to cross — and I caught my own eyes going back to the small screen beside the wheel.

When I learned to drive, I was taught to check the proximity of the car ahead. I still do, always. But checking proximity carries something else with it: a quiet drive to follow it.

Following a map, or a car, triggers an expected behaviour. An oriented one. An expectation of reality. That expectation did not hold for the plane. It does not hold for the self-driving car that calculated an accident-free course, or for the UI an AI laid out for a product I am building as if those were the options that existed. Every time, we assume some predefined conditions under which the machine is error-free, and then we treat the menu as the shape of the world.

The part that stays with me is who benefits. Bad actors are the ones who never rely on preselected options. If everyone else converges on the route they were offered — same stack, same defaults, same predicted safe course — then the road nobody offered is empty, and it is empty for them.

Human oversight is not a fence around the machine. It is the standing refusal to let the presented options become the only options.

Wrote about it with a small game. Three lanes: two lit SUGGESTED, one left dark. The lit ones move you forward. The dark one gives you nothing except a lower convergence.
