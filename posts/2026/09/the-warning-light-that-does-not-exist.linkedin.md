Somewhere between Pukë and Tropojë, on the narrow road into the Albanian Alps, a car overtook us. Two Polish travellers who liked taking pictures. We passed each other a dozen times that day, and every time we waved.

The last time, they were not waving hello. They were waving to tell me the tailpipe at the back of my car had detached.

My car was produced in 2015. Most of what is inside it is electronic, made to flag things when they break. It never flagged this. There is no sensor on the tailpipe. There was never going to be a light for it.

I drove slowly to Bajram Curri and a mechanic called Ismet fixed it for 25 euros. I still do not know what he did. I do not understand cars. I rely on people whose train of thought I cannot follow, and whose fix I could not check even if I watched.

That is the part that is not about cars. I can still write the typed functions by hand. But there is a strong pressure now to use the AI tools, because once they exist nothing else is justified: a feature that could take half a day cannot take three days anymore. The supplier has become the baseline.

And most of what I actually do with that speed is validate whether what came back is over-engineered or over-scoped. You ask for one thing and it returns something that has grown, and the only way out is to remove features and hope the backlog remembers them.

The risk is not that the light fails to come on. It was never going to. The risk is when nobody is left outside the system who can still notice what it does not sense — when trust in the supplier stops being a choice, because it is the only option left on the menu.

Human oversight is not a warning light. It is the willingness to keep looking under the car when nothing on the dashboard has asked you to.
