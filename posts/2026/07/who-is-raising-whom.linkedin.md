A colleague met my daughter on a call — ten months old, strapped to my chest in the kangaroo. She watched, the way she always does before she decides anything about a person. And then came the sentence I have not been able to put down since: you don't understand her, and therefore she might yell or cry.

Then I went back to work. Four terminals, somewhere between four and twenty-eight agents running at once. Ask an agent to copy the entire SQLite over to Rust — the source code right there, all of it — and it will hallucinate, and it will justify every change it asks to make. So we force it back to the structure that already works, the way a parent would. And when it enriches instead of copying, we read it as rebellion — which is what growing up looks like.

Dino Buzzati wrote a story where the young hunt the old through the city at night — until the leader of the mob, aged by his own hunt in a single night, turns around and finds a new pack of boys coming for him. The hunt does not end — the roles move. My daughter understands my emotions, of that I have no doubt; what she cannot yet do is verify my words, and I cannot verify hers. With the model it is the same exchange — and we are the ones who decide which side of the table we are sitting on.

That is what human oversight is underneath all the frameworks: not a fence around a kid, but a standing decision about who holds the label — renewed every time we force the structure back, or let the enrichment stand.
