A person applies for a loan. Rejected. They ask why — and there is no one who can answer. Not the loan officer, even if he wanted to, even if the law says he should. Nobody.

The system behind the verdict was built out of perfectly reasonable choices: someone studied the patterns of users who might default, someone chose a model, someone decided which data validates it and which metrics to watch while it runs. Every choice had an owner. The rejection has none.

𝐓𝐡𝐞 𝐦𝐨𝐝𝐞𝐥 𝐢𝐬 𝐚𝐜𝐜𝐮𝐫𝐚𝐭𝐞. 𝐓𝐡𝐞 𝐯𝐞𝐫𝐝𝐢𝐜𝐭 𝐢𝐬 𝐟𝐢𝐧𝐚𝐥. 𝐍𝐨𝐛𝐨𝐝𝐲 𝐜𝐚𝐧 𝐭𝐞𝐥𝐥 𝐲𝐨𝐮 𝐰𝐡𝐲.

That is the gap Reid Blackman describes in Ethical Machines. ML catches patterns that would take us — if we were lucky — enormous effort to see, and we explain the world through easy patterns; it works in exactly the opposite direction. The more accurate it gets, the further its reasons drift from anything we could retell. I feel it in my own work: sometimes I cannot retell why the model proposed what it proposed. And it was still right.

I started out with human reviewers who explained every rejected line of my code — a local bias, a local explanation. That is what human oversight still is: not out-predicting the machine, but staying the one who can say why.

Wrote about it with a small game where you play the loan officer: the black box has already decided, you call its verdict before the stamp lands, a match means you have a why to give the applicant — and mid-game the model retrains overnight, the accuracy holds, and the pattern you spent ten applicants learning quietly dies: https://arra.blog/posts/2026/07/nobody-can-tell-you-why.html
