This week an AI session filed a bug against something that was already done.

Three or four days of work on a feature. All the edge cases designed, all the scenarios of it going wrong thought through. Then a new session, which had forgotten everything the previous one and I had decided, came back and said one of those decisions was a bug. And the explanation was so bad that I could not understand it.

My instinct said, strongly, do not trust this. But instinct is not a decision. For a moment my brain wanted to give up on having fights with AI systems at all.

What saved me is that, as a developer, I have a button. Discard all changes. I pressed it.

Around the same days I watched a talk by IBM's lead for trustworthy AI, on why a human in the loop is not a governance strategy. She takes what we rely on when we trust a person and asks what each one becomes when the person is a model. Reliability becomes explainability: why did it produce this output, for this person, today. Can you predict its reasoning. That time, I could not.

And she gives four things a human in the loop needs before it is one. Visibility: can the person see the reasoning, not only the output. Authority: can the person stop the process, and does the button work. The right measurement: does the person know what done looks like. Feedback loops: do the rules survive to the next session, or does somebody write them again every time.

I had three of the four. I had the button, I knew what done looked like, I had rules even if they were forgotten. What I was missing was the first. And without it, the button was the only thing left, and I pressed it on instinct.

Human oversight is not the button. It is the other three things that let you press the button for a reason.

Wrote about it with a small game. Six decisions you made over four days, on six tiles. Then the sessions arrive as cards: this one is a bug, here is why, and if you do nothing it applies itself. Some are real. Most have forgotten. You can keep yours, accept, ask why, or write the rule down, while your own memory of what you decided fades tile by tile. At the end, the game puts in a box which of the four you had: https://arra.blog/posts/2026/10/discard-all-changes.html
